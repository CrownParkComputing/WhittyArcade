Seven ways

How many players?

Most emulators have one answer to that. Pick a game here and WhittyArcade asks how you want to play it, then offers only what that particular board can genuinely do — including two people sat at two different computers.

On your own 1 player Pick a cabinet, press a button, you are in it. No launcher standing in front of the launcher.
Take turns 2 players · one screen The original alternating game. One of you dies, the other steps up, exactly as the cabinet always did it. Galaga · Galaxian · Moon Cresta · UniWar S · Phoenix · Pac-Mania · Shinobi · Ghosts'n Goblins
Both at once 2 players · one screen Two sets of controls live together, for the games built that way. Player two gets an aiming sight of their own. Virtua Cop · Virtua Cop 2
Split the monitor 2 players · a view each One wide screen becomes two player views, each keeping the cabinet's real shape inside its own border rather than being stretched to fit.
A monitor each 2 players · 2 displays Two displays plugged in? Put a player on each. On a machine with one, the option is still listed — greyed out, so you know it exists.
Across two computers 2 players · 2 machines Open it on both. They find each other, sort out who is player one, and narrow the list to games you both own. One of you picks; the other machine starts itself.
Linked cabinets 2 boards · the real link Two whole arcade boards wired together through the link hardware their cabinets actually used — side by side on one PC, or one on each of two. Sega Rally · Ridge Racer 2 · Rave Racer

Attract mode, several times over

A whole wall of cabinets, all awake

Pick several games and they go up side by side across the display — not thumbnails or videos, but live boards running their own attract modes, waiting for a coin. The one you are playing grows; the rest divide what is left and stay quiet, so a wall sounds like one cabinet rather than an arcade at closing time.

How many you get is measured from your machine and your monitor rather than fixed at some number: a big PC on a wide screen gets a bigger wall, and a cabinet that cannot keep up says so instead of quietly running slow.

Swap one column for a different game and the cabinets either side never stop. Each can be framed in its own cabinet's bezel artwork, fetched once for the games you own and kept on your machine afterwards.

Open the coin door

The switches the operator had

Everything is reachable from the sofa with a pad — the shelf, the sorting, the settings, the pause menu. The keyboard is there if you want it, not because you need it.

A shelf of cover art

Every game is a card carrying its own arcade title screen — nothing but the artwork, no caption repeating what the picture already says.

Flip through the hardware

Turn the shelf into a page per board, or per publisher. Push past the end of a page to turn it, and an info button says what the board actually was.

Hall of fame

An arcade ranking screen where the rows sweep in one at a time and the champion's medal pulses. Real scores, read out of the games themselves.

Coinage, lives, difficulty

Pause anything and the cabinet's own DIP switches are right there — the settings the arcade owner used to open the coin door for.

Your controls, your way

Bind anything to anything: globally, per board, or per game. Pads are remembered by device, so plugging the same one back in brings its settings with it.

Bring your own ROMs

None ship here and none are linked. It asks where your collection already lives and reads it where it sits — nothing imported, copied or repacked.

Ready?

Builds for Windows and Linux, with everything they need to run. Nothing to install — unpack it and switch it on.
